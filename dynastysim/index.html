One pot vs a separate trust for each branch

One pot (your current design): every living descendant is paid each year, with older generations getting more.

Branch trusts: the money is split equally among the starting children, and each share is held for that person and pays only that person. When someone dies, their share passes equally to their living children. If they have none, it goes equally to the other shares. Grandchildren receive nothing until their parent dies.

Lifetime trusts, then outright: the same split and the same payouts to each child, but when a child dies the remaining money is paid out to their living children in a lump sum and the trust for that branch ends. After that the money is theirs, outside the trust, so later generations show nothing from the trust.

Both use the same returns and the same simulated families in every run, so the differences come from the structure alone. Gen 1 payouts are the same under branch trusts and lifetime trusts, so the dashed lines also describe lifetime trusts for generation 1. Branch trusts always use the percentage-of-value rule, and the starting split is among generation 1 (or among generation 2 if generation 1 is empty).

Per-member columns describe individual people across all simulations. Member counts are divided by the number of simulations, so they are averages per simulation. The generation-total percentiles add up everything a generation receives within each simulation and rank only the simulations where that generation has at least one person counted, so the 10th percentile is a poor outcome for a generation that exists. The share of simulations where it has no one counted is shown separately: the family died out or never reached that generation, or (in Completed lives only) none of its members has died by the last simulated year. "Invested" = every payout invested like the trust until death. Completed lives counts only people whose lives end within the simulated years, which understates late generations. So far counts everyone born by the last year, using payouts received up to that point or up to death. Existing family members are counted from today.

A plain-language guide

What is a dynasty trust?

A dynasty trust is a trust meant to last for many generations. A family puts money into it once. The trust invests the money and pays some of it out to the family each year: first to children, then grandchildren, great-grandchildren and so on, for as long as the law allows and the money lasts. Because the money stays in the trust instead of being inherited, it can avoid estate tax each time a generation dies.

What this tool does

It plays out the future thousands of times. In each run it invents a family (who is born, who has children, when people die) and a market history (good years, bad years, inflation), then follows the trust year by year: investment growth, taxes and fees go in and out, and the yearly payout is shared among everyone alive. Looking at all the runs together shows what usually happens, and how good or bad things could get.

How to use it

  1. Describe the trust. Under Trust, enter how much goes in and how much is paid out each year. A payout of 1% to 3% of the trust's value is typical for a trust meant to last.
  2. Describe the family. Under Family, enter the ages of the children (and grandchildren, if any) and how many children each descendant tends to have.
  3. Pick an investment outlook. Use the Conservative, Base or Optimistic buttons at the top of the settings. This is the setting that matters most.
  4. Read the results. Start with the summary sentence and the cards at the top of Overview. Then look at the payout chart to see what each generation might receive.
  5. Try changes. Press Save scenario, change one setting, and compare in the Compare tab. "Generate a random family" shows one possible future in detail.

How to read the results

  • Today's dollars. Amounts are adjusted for inflation, so $100,000 in year 80 buys what $100,000 buys today.
  • Median. The middle outcome: half of the runs did better and half did worse.
  • 10th and 90th percentile. A bad case and a good case. In 1 run out of 10 things turned out worse than the 10th percentile, and in 1 out of 10 better than the 90th. The shaded band on the charts is the range between them.
  • Log scale. On the charts each gridline is ten times the one below, so very large and very small amounts fit on one picture. Untick "Log scale" for an ordinary scale.
  • Family line dies out. The share of runs where no descendants are left. The trust would then end, usually under the terms set in the trust document.

What matters most

  • Investment return versus payout. If the trust earns more after inflation, taxes and fees than it pays out, it grows. If it pays out more, it shrinks. Over 100 years or more, a difference of one percentage point changes the outcome many times over.
  • Family growth. The payout is shared by everyone alive. A family that doubles every generation splits the money among many more people, so each person's share falls even when the trust grows.
  • Taxes and fees. Small yearly costs add up over long periods, just like returns.

Key terms

Real and nominal
Nominal amounts are actual dollars. Real amounts take out inflation. A 7.5% nominal return with 2.5% inflation is about a 4.9% real return.
Distribution rate
The share of the trust's value paid out each year.
Pot trust and branch trusts
A pot trust is one shared fund for the whole family. Branch trusts split the money into a separate trust for each child's line. The Compare tab shows how the choice changes who gets paid.
GST tax and exemption
The generation-skipping transfer tax is a 40% federal tax on wealth that passes to grandchildren or later generations. Each person can shelter a set amount from it ($15 million in 2026, rising with inflation after that). This tool assumes the whole trust is sheltered. If you put in more than the exemption covers, real results would be worse than shown.
Rule against perpetuities
A state law that limits how long a trust can last. Some states let trusts last forever or for several hundred years; others limit them to around 90 years. Use "Years simulated" to match your state.
Monte Carlo
Running a model many times with random inputs to see the range of possible results.
Tax drag
The yearly cost of taxes on the trust's income and gains, shown as a percentage of its value.

What it doesn't do

It doesn't give advice, forecast the future or replace a lawyer. It doesn't model the GST tax on non-exempt money, state income tax on the trust itself, changes in tax law, spouses as beneficiaries, or the trustee's discretion to pay more to someone in need. Use it to understand the trade-offs, then take your questions to an estate attorney and a tax advisor.

About, method, limits and sources

What it does. It simulates an irrevocable trust that pays one annual distribution to all living descendants, with each person's share weighted by generation or age, and shows how the trust and each generation's payouts could turn out across thousands of simulated families and market histories.

How it works. Each simulation builds a random family (births, deaths and the number of descendants by generation, with optional trends in family size and life expectancy for people born later) and runs the trust year by year. Investment returns are drawn at random each year (Monte Carlo), generated by a layered model whose returns change over time (Layered), held constant (Simple), or taken from real market history by picking a random start year and running forward in order (Historical). Historical data can be 16 developed countries from 1871 to 2020, either averaged each year like a world index fund or one country at a time, or US data from 1928 to 2023. Payouts follow the rule you choose. Taxes are either a flat drag or a detailed estimate of trust-level tax on retained dividends and on gains from shares sold, with an optional beneficiary-level income tax. Percentiles show the 10th, 50th (median) and 90th percentile outcomes across simulations. All dollars are in today's dollars unless you switch to nominal in the single-family view.

Layered return model. Each simulation stacks four layers, all in real (after-inflation) terms. (1) Centuries: the simulation draws its own long-run return around the one you enter, optionally drifting by a set amount per century. (2) Decades: the expected return wanders around that long-run return in eras with a set half-life; when prices fall, expected future returns rise, so part of a fall is made up later (the “recovery strength” setting, following the predictive-system approach of Pástor and Stambaugh). (3) Years: markets switch between normal years and crisis years (about one year in five; crisis spells average about three years) with higher volatility and lower returns, plus optional slow shifts in volatility over decades. (4) Rare events: disasters cause permanent losses that are not made up afterward. Inflation varies from year to year and rises in crisis years. The long-run return you enter already allows for disaster losses, so the median compounded return stays close to it. With “treat these settings as uncertain” on, each simulation also draws its own era length, swing size, recovery strength and disaster chance, which approximates simulating from a Bayesian posterior.

How the Layered defaults were fitted. The original hand-set values served as a prior. The data are yearly real stock returns from 1871 to 2020 for 16 developed countries, averaged each year like a world index fund (the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor data described under Sources). The fit picks the settings that best balance two things: matching nine features of the data (yearly volatility, how lopsided and fat-tailed returns are, how often a year falls 20 points below average, how 5-, 10- and 20-year returns compare with yearly ones, and how calm and turbulent years cluster), and staying close to the prior. Each feature is weighted by how precisely the data measure it, using a 10-year block bootstrap. This is a penalized simulated method of moments, which gives the most likely settings under the prior. The data moved the volatility settings (normal years from 14% to 10%, crisis years from 28% to 17%), the disaster settings (1.3% a year, with an average permanent loss of 32%), and the size of slow shifts in volatility (larger than assumed). The data say little about how long eras last, how far expected returns swing or how strongly prices recover, so those stay close to the prior. The uncertainty in the long-run return (1.1 points) is the sampling error of the 150-year average. There is no clear trend in returns (+0.5 points per century, give or take 2.4), so the default trend stays at zero. Inflation persistence (0.7 a year) and the size of yearly inflation shocks (1.6 points) were fitted to the countries' median inflation. The long-run return itself is not fitted, because it is your main input. History averaged about 6.1% a year after inflation across the 16 countries.

Markets missing from the data. The historical data covers only countries whose markets survived and that are rich today. Two of the roughly 20 to 25 stock markets that existed around 1900 were wiped out (Russia's in 1917 and China's in 1949), about a 0.07% chance per market per year, and others went through long closures or deep losses. Studies that include every market find the typical one did worse than the survivors. The tool adjusts for this in three ways. Historical runs subtract an allowance from stock returns each year (0.5 points by default, adjustable; estimates range from about 0.1 to over 1 point). Runs that follow one country at a time also give that market a 0.07% yearly chance of going to zero. The Layered model gives the world index the same yearly chance of losing one of its roughly 16 markets for good, which costs about 0.1 points a year. The return presets (5%, 7.5% and 8.75% nominal, or about 2.4%, 4.9% and 6.1% after 2.5% inflation) sit at or below the historical average. The base case is about 0.7 points below history after the 0.5-point allowance, leaving room for today's higher stock prices relative to earnings, which have historically been followed by lower returns.

Main limits.

  • The long-run average return is the biggest driver of results and cannot be known. Over 100 to 250 years a difference of a point or two changes outcomes by an order of magnitude. Use the conservative and optimistic setups and the uncertain-average-return option.
  • In the Monte Carlo model yearly returns are independent of each other, while real markets show longer cycles. The Layered model adds eras, crises and disasters with settings fitted to 150 years of data from 16 countries, but 150 years is not enough to measure how long eras last or how strongly prices recover, so those settings remain mostly assumptions.
  • The trust is assumed to be fully exempt from the federal generation-skipping transfer (GST) tax, meaning it is funded within the donor's GST exemption ($15M per person in 2026) with the exemption allocated to it. A trust that is partly or wholly non-exempt would owe a 40% GST tax as wealth passes to grandchildren and later generations; that tax is not modeled.
  • Taxes use flat rates you enter, not progressive brackets. The trust is assumed to be based in a state that does not tax trust income; beneficiaries' state income tax is optional. Spouses are not beneficiaries.
  • Fertility and life expectancy can trend steadily over time, but the trends are straight lines you choose, not forecasts, and they apply to everyone in the family alike.
  • Some figures (payout medians, lifetime percentiles) are read from compact histograms and can differ from exact values by about 1%. Confidence ranges, when shown, reflect only the number of simulations.
  • Defaults for returns, volatility, yields and tax rates are illustrative assumptions, not forecasts.

Sources. Historical annual returns for the S&P 500 (with dividends), 10-year US Treasury bonds and US inflation, 1928 to 2023, are from Aswath Damodaran, NYU Stern, Historical Returns on Stocks, Bonds and Bills. The US stock data is likely optimistic for a global stock index. Stock and government bond total returns and consumer prices for 16 countries (Australia, Belgium, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands, Norway, Portugal, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, the United Kingdom and the United States), 1871 to 2020 where available, are from the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database, release 6, described in Jordà, Knoll, Kuvshinov, Schularick and Taylor, “The Rate of Return on Everything, 1870–2015,” Quarterly Journal of Economics 134(3), 2019. Years with missing data (mostly around the world wars) are skipped, so a run that reaches a gap jumps to a new random start. The database covers countries whose markets survived; markets that were wiped out, such as Russia's in 1917 and China's in 1949, are not included.
